Exploring the Psychology of Belonging

Alfred Adler (1870–1937) was an Austrian physician, psychotherapist, and the founder of Individual Psychology...

His work shifted psychology away from isolated individuals toward community, cooperation and purpose.

Biography & Ideas

From Vienna to Individual Psychology

Alfred Adler was born in Vienna and originally trained as a medical doctor. Early in his career he was part of Sigmund Freud’s psychoanalytic circle, but he later disagreed with Freud’s focus on instinctual drives. Adler argued that people are shaped by their goals, their sense of belonging, and the social environments in which they live.

He developed Individual Psychology, a holistic approach that sees the person as an indivisible whole—mind, body, and community. Rather than asking only “Where does this come from?”, Adler also asked “What purpose does this behavior serve?”

Inferiority, Striving, and Social Interest

Adler introduced the idea of the inferiority complex and showed how feelings of weakness or inadequacy can lead people either toward growth or toward unhealthy compensation. He believed that psychological health is closely tied to social interest: the capacity to feel connected to others and to contribute to the common good.

According to Adler, we are not prisoners of our childhood. Instead, we creatively interpret our early experiences and use them to build a unique “life style” — a pattern of beliefs, goals, and habits that guides how we meet the tasks of work, friendship, and love.

Legacy and Influence

Adler’s ideas influenced counseling, education, family therapy, community psychology, and modern approaches to positive mental health. Concepts such as encouragement, democratic parenting, birth order, and goal-oriented behavior all trace part of their history back to his work.

Today, Adlerian psychology continues to inspire therapists, teachers, and community workers who believe that every person is capable of growth, cooperation, and meaningful contribution to society.

Selected Works

A selection of Alfred Adler’s most influential books and publications.

The Neurotic Constitution

1917

Early work outlining Adler’s ideas on neurosis, compensation, and the subjective logic people use to protect a fragile sense of self.

The Practice and Theory of Individual Psychology

1920s

A systematic presentation of Individual Psychology, describing how family dynamics, birth order, and personal goals shape personality.

Understanding Human Nature

1927

Accessible lectures for the general public, explaining how feelings of inferiority and striving for significance affect everyday life.

What Life Should Mean to You

1931

A practical guide encouraging readers to develop courage, cooperation, and social interest as foundations for a meaningful life.

Superiority and Social Interest

1964 (posthumous)

A collection of later writings that deepen Adler’s reflections on power, community, and the ethical direction of human striving.
